A design objective can contain products, ratios and fractional powers and still belong to a globally tractable optimization class. The decisive issue is the algebraic pattern, not whether the printed expression looks nonlinear. Geometric programming exploits positive sums of positive power-law terms. The positive domain makes logarithms available, and those logarithms expose convexity.

Use a dimensionless design with two positive variables $x,y$ and objective

$$f(x,y)=x+y+\frac1{xy},\qquad x>0,\ y>0.$$

The first two terms charge for increasing the two design resources. The last term penalizes making their product too small. These terms create an interior balance. Later impose $x\le1/2$ and see how a physical limit changes the optimum and its dual certificate. This is an original teaching construction; it avoids treating uncertain caption exponents as verified engineering data.

1. Positivity is a mathematical requirement 00:19

A monomial in the geometric-programming sense is

$$m(x)=c\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{a_i},\qquad c>0,\quad x_i>0,\quad a_i\in\mathbb R.$$

Real exponents may be negative or fractional. This differs from the polynomial-algebra convention of nonnegative integer exponents. A posynomial is a finite sum of such monomials, each with a positive coefficient. The word does not mean merely “a polynomial with a positive value.” The expression $x-y+3$ may be positive on a chosen box yet has a negative coefficient and is not a posynomial in the stated variables.

Strict positivity matters. The term $x^{-1/2}$ is not defined as a finite real number at zero. The logarithmic coordinate $\log x$ also excludes zero. Replacing the domain by $x\ge0$ without qualification changes both the function and its transformation. If a physical variable can vanish, its zero boundary must be handled separately or the model must use a justified positive offset.

Monomials are closed under multiplication, division and real powers. Posynomials are closed under addition, multiplication and positive scaling, and division by a monomial preserves posynomial structure. Division by a general posynomial does not generally do so. These algebraic facts follow by collecting exponents and positive coefficients, and determine whether a proposed rearrangement preserves the problem class.

A standard geometric program, or GP, minimizes a posynomial $f_0(x)$ subject to posynomial inequalities $f_k(x)\le1$ and monomial equalities $h_\ell(x)=1$, all on $x_i>0$. A positive constant on the right can be absorbed into the coefficients. A posynomial bounded above by a monomial can be divided by that monomial. A lower bound on a monomial can be inverted. These transformations preserve GP structure because the denominator has a single power-law term.

The distinction among allowed inequality directions and equality forms is explicit in Boyd, Kim, Vandenberghe and Hassibi, §2. The name of the class is less important than verifying that every expression actually satisfies its positive-coefficient and domain contract.

For a dimensional physical model, logarithms should be applied to ratios $x_i/x_{i,\mathrm{ref}}$ against fixed positive reference units. Writing $\log x_i$ conventionally assumes numerical values in chosen units. Changing those units changes the coefficients consistently; it should not change the modeled physical design. A power law fitted empirically also carries a range of validity that algebra cannot extend automatically.

2. Logarithms expose a convex optimization problem 31:21

Set $z_i=\log x_i$, so $x_i=e^{z_i}$ and $z\in\mathbb R^n$. A monomial becomes $m(e^z)=\exp(\log c+a^\top z)$. Taking its logarithm gives an affine function. For a posynomial $f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^N c_jx^{a_j}$, define

$$F(z)=\log f(e^z) =\log\sum_{j=1}^N\exp(a_j^\top z+\log c_j).$$

Here $a_j\in\mathbb R^n$ is the exponent vector for term $j$, and $x^{a_j}$ abbreviates $\prod_i x_i^{a_{ij}}$. The logarithm is increasing, so minimizing $F$ is equivalent to minimizing $f$. Inequalities $f_k(x)\le1$ become $F_k(z)\le0$, while monomial equalities become affine equations.

The convexity mechanism can be proved directly. Let

$$w_j(z)=\frac{\exp(a_j^\top z+\log c_j)} {\sum_r\exp(a_r^\top z+\log c_r)},\qquad \bar a=\sum_jw_ja_j.$$

These positive weights sum to one. Differentiation yields $\nabla F=\bar a$ and

$$\nabla^2F=\sum_j w_j(a_j-\bar a)(a_j-\bar a)^\top.$$

For every vector $v$, the corresponding quadratic form is $\sum_jw_j[v^\top(a_j-\bar a)]^2\ge0$. Thus the Hessian is positive semidefinite. A logarithmic GP is a convex problem even when its original posynomial is not convex in ordinary coordinates. This proof also reveals possible flat directions: if every exponent has the same projection along $v$, the variance in that direction is zero.

For the running objective, $F(u,v)=\log(e^u+e^v+e^{-u-v})$, where $u=\log x$ and $v=\log y$. At $u=v=0$, all three terms are one and their exponent vectors $(1,0),(0,1),(-1,-1)$ average to zero. Therefore the transformed gradient vanishes, establishing a global minimum by convexity. Its value in original units is three at $x=y=1$.

Convexity does not by itself prove attainment or uniqueness. Minimizing the monomial $x$ over $x>0$ has infimum zero but no minimizer. A posynomial depending only on $xy$ can have a continuum of minimizers with that product fixed. Check growth, feasible bounds and effective exponent rank before asserting a finite unique design.

For numerical evaluation, compute log-sum-exp by subtracting the largest exponent: if $s_j=a_j^\top z+\log c_j$ and $s_{\max}=\max_js_j$, then $F=s_{\max}+\log\sum_j e^{s_j-s_{\max}}$. This identity avoids overflow from a large common exponent. It changes numerical representation, not the mathematical problem.

3. The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality supplies a lower bound 41:29

For positive $r_j$ and nonnegative weights $\delta_j$ summing to one, weighted arithmetic–geometric mean gives $\sum_j\delta_jr_j\ge\prod_jr_j^{\delta_j}$. One proof uses $\log t\le t-1$ for $t>0$, obtained by differentiating $t-1-\log t$ and identifying its minimum at one. Apply this inequality to normalized ratios and sum with the weights. Equality holds when all positively weighted $r_j$ coincide.

Let $U_j(x)=c_jx^{a_j}$ be the objective's monomial terms. For positive $\delta_j$ summing to one, use $r_j=U_j/\delta_j$:

$$f(x)=\sum_j\delta_j\frac{U_j(x)}{\delta_j} \ge\prod_j\left(\frac{c_j}{\delta_j}\right)^{\delta_j} \prod_i x_i^{\sum_j a_{ij}\delta_j}.$$

If the exponent-balance equations $\sum_j a_{ij}\delta_j=0$ hold for every variable, all powers of $x_i$ cancel. The remaining product

$$D(\delta)=\prod_j\left(\frac{c_j}{\delta_j}\right)^{\delta_j}$$

is a global lower bound on $f$. Maximizing this bound over feasible weights gives the classical unconstrained GP dual. Zero weights can be included by continuous limits, with factors interpreted through $\delta\log(c/\delta)\to0$ as $\delta\downarrow0$.

At an attained positive stationary point $x_*$, define $\delta_j=U_j(x_*)/f(x_*)$. Normalization holds because the terms sum to the objective. Multiplying $\partial f/\partial x_i=0$ by $x_i$ gives $\sum_j a_{ij}U_j(x_*)=0$, hence exponent balance. Moreover $U_j(x_*)/\delta_j=f(x_*)$ for every term, so the arithmetic–geometric mean bound is tight. This constructs a matching lower bound and proves global optimality rather than merely observing a stationary point.

For $f=x+y+1/(xy)$, exponent balance requires $\delta_1=\delta_3$ and $\delta_2=\delta_3$. Together with normalization, all weights equal $1/3$. The bound is $3^{1/3}3^{1/3}3^{1/3}=3$. At $(1,1)$ the objective attains three, yielding a complete primal-dual certificate. No numerical optimizer is required for that proof.

4. Recovering a design and interpreting degree of difficulty 14:32 · 20:21

When optimal dual weights are positive and the lower bound is tight, $U_j(x_*)=\delta_jf_*$. Taking logarithms gives linear equations

$$a_j^\top z_* = \log\left(\frac{\delta_jf_*}{c_j}\right).$$

Solve these for logarithmic coordinates, then exponentiate. The system can be overdetermined but consistent. Its rank determines whether the coordinates are unique. A numerical least-squares solution should be checked by substituting back into every monomial equation and original constraint, rather than silently accepting inconsistent rounded data.

The classical degree of difficulty count is $N-n-1$ for $N$ monomial terms and $n$ variables. It estimates how many free dual weights remain after normalization and exponent balance when those equations have full independent rank. The actual affine dimension is $N-\operatorname{rank}M$, where $M$ stacks the normalization row and exponent-balance rows, before nonnegativity restrictions. Counting equations is not a substitute for checking independence and feasibility.

A positive degree of difficulty does not mean the primal has multiple optima. The one-variable function $x+1/x+1$ has three terms, one variable and count one, yet its unique minimum is at $x=1$. The extra constant creates a free dual parameter before optimization; maximizing the dual still selects its optimum.

A negative count also does not make the optimization problem invalid. The two-variable function $x+1/x$ has two terms and count minus one, yet it is minimized at $x=1$ with arbitrary positive $y$. The variable $y$ does not appear. Redundant coordinates make the raw count misleading. The logarithmic convex formulation handles the problem without a special difficulty-count exception.

Even a zero count does not guarantee an attained primal solution: feasibility of the resulting nonnegative dual weights, rank and primal recovery all matter. In this edition the count describes a feature of the classical dual calculation, rather than serving as a theorem about uniqueness of every GP design.

5. Constraints add dual groups and multipliers 00:19

Write objective terms as $U_{0j}=c_{0j}x^{a_{0j}}$ and terms of constraint $k$ as $U_{kj}=c_{kj}x^{a_{kj}}$, with $f_k=\sum_jU_{kj}\le1$. Introduce nonnegative weights $\delta_{kj}$ and group sums $\lambda_k=\sum_j\delta_{kj}$ for $k\ge1$. Normalize only the objective group: $\sum_j\delta_{0j}=1$.

Weighted arithmetic–geometric mean applied separately to each group gives

$$f_0\ge\prod_j\left(\frac{U_{0j}}{\delta_{0j}}\right)^{\delta_{0j}}, \qquad f_k^{\lambda_k}\ge\prod_j\left(\frac{\lambda_kU_{kj}}{\delta_{kj}}\right)^{\delta_{kj}}.$$

Since every feasible $f_k\le1$, the product $f_0\prod_k f_k^{\lambda_k}$ is at most $f_0$. Combining the lower bounds and imposing total exponent balance $\sum_{k,j}a_{kj}\delta_{kj}=0$ yields

$$f_0(x)\ge D(\delta)= \left[\prod_{k,j}\left(\frac{c_{kj}}{\delta_{kj}}\right)^{\delta_{kj}}\right] \left[\prod_{k=1}^m\lambda_k^{\lambda_k}\right].$$

This is a feasible-design lower bound for ordinary GP inequalities. Its log form is numerically preferable to multiplying many large or small factors. Inactive groups can have $\lambda_k=0$ and zero term weights, interpreted by limits. Equality with a feasible primal objective supplies a certificate; more general strong-duality statements require the usual regularity conditions of the convex transformed problem.

Impose $x\le1/2$ in the running model, written $2x\le1$. For any fixed $x$, minimizing $y+1/(xy)$ gives $y=1/\sqrt x$, by differentiation or arithmetic–geometric mean. The reduced objective is $\psi(x)=x+2/\sqrt x$. Its derivative is $1-x^{-3/2}$, negative for $x<1$. Therefore the constrained minimum uses $x_*=1/2$, $y_*=\sqrt2$, with value $f_*=1/2+2\sqrt2$.

Let $v=f_*$. Objective weights are $\delta_1=(1/2)/v$ and $\delta_2=\delta_3=\sqrt2/v$. The constraint has one term and weight $\lambda=\delta_3-\delta_1>0$. Its exponent is $(1,0)$, so total balance is $(\delta_1-\delta_3+\lambda,\delta_2-\delta_3)=(0,0)$. The logarithmic dual value reduces to $-\sum_{j=1}^3\delta_j\log\delta_j+\lambda\log2$. The lab verifies that exponentiating it equals $v$, independently certifying the constrained design.

6. Reversed posynomial inequalities change the problem class 36:30

A lower bound on a monomial is harmless: $x^{-1}y^2\ge3$ becomes $3xy^{-2}\le1$. A lower bound on a posynomial sum is different. The set $x+y\ge2$ becomes $e^u+e^v\ge2$ in logarithmic coordinates. It need not be convex.

Take positive designs $(x,y)=(2,1/4)$ and $(1/4,2)$. Both satisfy the inequality. Their midpoint in log coordinates maps to their componentwise geometric mean $(\sqrt{1/2},\sqrt{1/2})$, whose sum is $\sqrt2<2$. The transformed midpoint is infeasible. Thus the log-coordinate feasible set is nonconvex, even though every coefficient is positive.

Lecture 36 develops sign-based algebra for mixed inequality directions. Those generalizations must not inherit ordinary GP's convex global guarantee merely because a sign is inserted in a dual expression. A proposed generalized dual needs its own weak-duality proof and qualifications; stationary multiplier equations alone cannot replace them. The safe practice is to test whether each reversed constraint is monomial-reducible and otherwise identify the model as outside standard GP.

Similarly, a posynomial equality such as $x+y=1$ is not an allowed monomial equality. Its logarithmic equality is a curved surface rather than an affine subspace. A model may admit another exact reformulation, but that requires an argument. Maximizing a general posynomial is also not equivalent to standard GP by taking its reciprocal, because a reciprocal of a sum is not generally a posynomial.

The laboratory solves the running problem in logarithmic coordinates, checks both analytic optima, and constructs the constrained dual certificate. A direct midpoint test verifies the reversed-inequality counterexample. The script accepts no external data, performs no file or network operations and checks numerical answers against independently derived values. It is an illustrative implementation; stable general solvers should use log-sum-exp and complete feasibility/residual checks for large models.

Six worked problems

Exercise 1 — Classify an expression

Which expressions are posynomials on $x,y>0$: $3x^{-1/2}y^2$, $x+y-1$, and $(x+y)/\sqrt{xy}$?

Worked solution

The first is a monomial with positive coefficient three and real exponents. The second has a negative constant coefficient, so it is not a posynomial in the stated form. The third expands to $x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}+x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}$, a sum of two positive monomials. Division by the denominator is permitted because $\sqrt{xy}$ is a monomial.

Exercise 2 — Transform a constraint

Put $x^2+y\le\sqrt x$ into standard GP and logarithmic form.

Worked solution

Divide by the positive monomial $\sqrt x$ to obtain $x^{3/2}+yx^{-1/2}\le1$. With $u=\log x,v=\log y$, the inequality is $\log(e^{3u/2}+e^{v-u/2})\le0$. Its left side is log-sum-exp of affine functions, hence convex by the Hessian argument. Dividing by a positive quantity preserves the inequality direction.

Exercise 3 — Construct the unconstrained certificate

Derive the dual lower bound for $x+y+1/(xy)$.

Worked solution

Exponent balance gives $\delta_1-\delta_3=0$ and $\delta_2-\delta_3=0$. Normalization fixes each to $1/3$. Weighted arithmetic–geometric mean then gives $f\ge\prod_j(1/(1/3))^{1/3}=3$. Equality requires $3x=3y=3/(xy)$, hence $x=y$ and $x^3=1$. The positive solution is $(1,1)$ and it attains the bound.

Exercise 4 — Recover the constrained optimum

Solve the same problem under $x\le1/2$ without a numerical optimizer.

Worked solution

At fixed $x$, $y+1/(xy)\ge2/\sqrt x$, with equality at $y=1/\sqrt x$. The remaining function $x+2x^{-1/2}$ decreases on $(0,1/2]$ because its derivative is $1-x^{-3/2}<0$. Therefore $x=1/2,y=\sqrt2$ and value $1/2+2\sqrt2$. This derivation checks the full positive domain and does not assume a stationary point exists in the constrained interior.

Exercise 5 — Interpret a difficulty count

Why does $x+1/x+1$ disprove the claim that positive degree of difficulty implies multiple primal optima?

Worked solution

There are three terms and one variable, so the count is $3-1-1=1$. Yet $x+1/x\ge2$ for $x>0$, with equality only at $x=1$. Adding one gives a unique minimum three. The count concerns freedom in the dual equality system before optimization, not the number of optimal primal designs.

Exercise 6 — Diagnose a reversed sum

Can the convex-GP guarantee be applied directly to $x+y\ge2$?

Worked solution

No. The two feasible designs $(2,.25)$ and $(.25,2)$ have log midpoint mapping to $(\sqrt{.5},\sqrt{.5})$. Its sum is $\sqrt2<2$, so the log-transformed feasible set is nonconvex. Inverting the whole sum produces $1/(x+y)\le1/2$, whose reciprocal-sum expression is not a posynomial. A different reformulation or a nonconvex method needs its own justification.

"""Geometric programming: primal solution and independent dual certificate."""
import json

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize


def main():
    # min x + y + 1/(x*y), x,y>0, with x<=1/2 in the constrained case.
    def objective(z):
        x, y = np.exp(z)
        return x + y + 1 / (x * y)

    free = minimize(objective, [1., -1.], method='BFGS',
                    options={'gtol': 1e-7})
    assert np.allclose(np.exp(free.x), [1, 1], atol=1e-6)
    assert np.isclose(free.fun, 3)
    constrained = minimize(objective, [-1., 0.], method='SLSQP',
                           constraints=[dict(type='ineq', fun=lambda z:
                                             -np.log(2) - z[0])],
                           options={'ftol': 1e-12})
    assert constrained.success
    x, y = np.exp(constrained.x)
    value = .5 + 2 * np.sqrt(2)
    assert np.allclose([x, y], [.5, np.sqrt(2)], atol=1e-6)
    assert np.isclose(constrained.fun, value)
    delta = np.array([.5, np.sqrt(2), np.sqrt(2)]) / value
    multiplier = delta[2] - delta[0]
    # Exponent balance: [1,0], [0,1], [-1,-1], [1,0].
    exponents = np.array([[1., 0.], [0., 1.], [-1., -1.], [1., 0.]])
    all_delta = np.r_[delta, multiplier]
    assert np.allclose(exponents.T @ all_delta, 0)
    log_dual = -np.sum(delta * np.log(delta)) + multiplier * np.log(2)
    assert np.isclose(np.exp(log_dual), value)
    assert np.isclose(delta.sum(), 1)
    # A posynomial superlevel set need not be convex in log coordinates.
    a, b = np.log([2., .25]), np.log([.25, 2.])
    midpoint = (a + b) / 2
    assert np.exp(a).sum() >= 2 and np.exp(b).sum() >= 2
    assert np.exp(midpoint).sum() < 2
    print(json.dumps(dict(unconstrained_point=np.exp(free.x).tolist(),
                          unconstrained_value=float(free.fun),
                          constrained_point=[float(x), float(y)],
                          constrained_value=value, dual_weights=delta.tolist(),
                          constraint_weight=float(multiplier),
                          dual_value=float(np.exp(log_dual)))))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
