12. Feasible directions, cutting planes, and penalty paths

A calibration setting with excellent loss can violate a temperature limit. Rejecting every infeasible trial is one response; allowing violations temporarily while charging for them is another. A third approach keeps every accepted point strictly inside the safe region and approaches its boundary gradually. These strategies differ in what they promise during computation, not merely in how their final answers are printed.

Let $x\in\mathbb R^n$ be the decision vector, $f(x)$ a scalar loss, $g_j(x)\le0$ the $m$ inequality constraints, and $h_k(x)=0$ the $p$ equality constraints. Define the feasible set $F$ by all these restrictions. An inequality is active at a feasible $x$ when $g_j(x)=0$, and inactive when its value is strictly negative. Activity describes geometry at a point; it does not mean that an inactive constraint can be discarded permanently.

We will use two deliberately small constructions. The scalar problem $\min x$ subject to $x\ge5$ exposes exact penalty and barrier paths. The curved constraint $x_1^2+x_2^2\le1$ exposes the distinction between a tangent direction and an actually feasible step. These examples are original teaching calculations tied to the lecture's methods.

1. Linearization describes a local cone 00:17 · 26:26

At a differentiable feasible point $x$, a direction $d$ is a strict descent direction if $\nabla f(x)^\top d<0$. Taylor expansion gives $f(x+\alpha d)=f(x)+\alpha\nabla f(x)^\top d+o(\alpha)$, so sufficiently small positive $\alpha$ decreases the loss. The statement concerns the objective; it says nothing yet about feasibility.

For an active inequality, linearized feasibility requires $\nabla g_j(x)^\top d\le0$. For an equality it requires $\nabla h_k(x)^\top d=0$. If all active inequalities decrease strictly and the functions are differentiable, a sufficiently short straight step remains feasible for those inequalities. Inactive inequalities also remain satisfied locally by continuity. Equalities and tangential active directions require more care because their second-order terms can decide feasibility.

At $x=(1,0)$ on the unit circle, take $d=(0,1)$. The gradient of $g(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2-1$ is $(2,0)$, so $\nabla g(x)^\top d=0$. Nevertheless,

$$g(x+\alpha d)=1+\alpha^2-1=\alpha^2>0$$

for every positive $\alpha$. A tangent vector belongs to the linearized cone but its straight ray immediately exits the disk. Following the curved path $(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ stays on the boundary. Feasible-direction algorithms therefore need an actual feasibility check, an inward direction, or a curved correction; checking only a first derivative is insufficient.

The same distinction explains equality handling. If $h(x)=0$ is nonlinear, a direction in the null space of its Jacobian preserves equality to first order, not exactly. A projection or restoration step may be required after moving. With affine equalities $Ax=b$, the condition $Ad=0$ preserves equality exactly, since $A(x+\alpha d)=b$ for every $\alpha$.

For a linear active boundary $x_1+x_2\ge4$, write $g(x)=4-x_1-x_2$. At $x=(1.45,2.55)$, minimizing $x_1^2+x_2^2$ gives gradient $(2.9,5.1)$. The direction $d=(1,-.7)$ satisfies objective derivative $2.9-3.57=-.67$ and constraint derivative $-1+.7=-.3$. It both improves the loss and moves inward. These are different inequalities; a direction that satisfies only the first can be an excellent infeasible move.

2. Choosing a usable direction is itself an optimization problem 38:41

A simplified direction-finding LP minimizes a scalar $\beta$ subject to

$$\nabla f(x)^\top d\le\beta,\qquad \nabla g_j(x)^\top d\le\beta\quad(j\in I(x)), \qquad J_h(x)d=0,\qquad -1\le d_i\le1,$$

where $I(x)$ is the active set and $J_h(x)$ is the equality Jacobian, whose rows are the gradients of the equality constraints. The Jacobian equation is omitted when there are no equalities. A negative optimum supplies strict objective decrease and strict inward motion for the selected inequalities, while preserving equality to first order. Nonlinear equalities still require the feasibility correction discussed above. Component bounds prevent an unbounded scaling of $d$. Multiplying an improving direction by arbitrarily large constants would otherwise make the derivative objective arbitrarily negative without solving the real step-length question.

Practical feasible-direction schemes modify the active set and introduce push factors to avoid poor tangential progress. The lecture develops that family through Zoutendijk's construction. The essential contract is unchanged: choose a direction, limit its length using the original constraints, and evaluate the new objective. The bounded LP is a local geometric subproblem, not a global certificate for a nonconvex problem.

A zero direction-subproblem improvement can indicate first-order constrained stationarity under suitable regularity, rather than global optimality. If a constraint has zero gradient at an isolated feasible point, the linearized geometry may be misleading. For instance $g(x)=x^2\le0$ permits only $x=0$, but its first-order constraint imposes no direction restriction there. Constraint qualifications in Chapter 9 explain why such singular cases cannot be treated by ordinary KKT reasoning without qualification.

After choosing $d$, solve a one-dimensional problem over the feasible values of $\alpha\ge0$. For affine constraints, allowable step lengths follow from linear inequalities in $\alpha$. For nonlinear constraints, evaluate them along the actual path or use a justified conservative bound. Starting in the interior does not permit ignoring constraints during line search; a long steepest-descent step can cross the boundary even when its initial directional derivative is favorable.

3. Convex supporting planes produce outer approximations 05:22

A differentiable convex function obeys the supporting-plane inequality

$$g(z)\ge g(x)+\nabla g(x)^\top(z-x).$$

If $g(z)\le0$, its affine lower bound is also at most zero. Thus the linear inequality $g(x)+\nabla g(x)^\top(z-x)\le0$ retains every feasible point. Several such cuts define an outer approximation: a region containing the true feasible set, possibly also many infeasible points. The cut is not guaranteed to put the next iterate outside the original feasible set; it merely permits that possibility.

Consider minimizing $-z$ subject to $z^2\le1$, with an initial bounding interval $[-2,2]$. The first relaxed solution is $z_0=2$. Linearizing $z^2-1$ there gives $3+4(z-2)\le0$, or $z\le1.25$. The next relaxed solution is 1.25. Its cut is $0.5625+2.5(z-1.25)\le0$, or $z\le1.025$. The process approaches the true solution one. The relaxed objectives $-2,-1.25,-1.025$ increase toward $-1$ because they are lower bounds for a minimization problem, while feasibility violations decrease.

For a nonlinear convex objective, a single Taylor approximation is generally inadequate. Introduce an epigraph variable $t$ representing an upper value for the objective and retain objective cuts

$$t\ge f(x_r)+\nabla f(x_r)^\top(z-x_r)$$

for previously sampled points $x_r$. Minimize $t$ subject to these cuts and the accumulated constraint cuts. The optimum is a lower bound on the true convex minimum over the bounded region. Any feasible evaluated point supplies an upper bound. Their difference gives a meaningful stopping quantity.

Merely finding a feasible minimizer of one linearized objective does not establish optimality of the nonlinear objective. For $f(z)=z^2$ on $[-1,1]$, linearizing at one produces objective $2z-1$, minimized at $z=-1$. That point is feasible but has objective one, whereas the true minimizer zero has objective zero. Retaining an epigraph model and an upper/lower bound comparison repairs the mistaken inference.

Convexity is what makes these cuts globally valid lower bounds. For a nonconvex function, a tangent can cut away good feasible points. Nonlinear equalities are also different: replacing them by tangent equalities does not give a global outer approximation. Sequential linear programming for general nonlinear programs consequently needs a trust region, restoration and acceptance rules, with local rather than automatic global conclusions.

4. Exterior penalties trade objective against violation 00:17

Define the nonnegative violation measure

$$P(x)=\sum_{k=1}^p h_k(x)^2+ \sum_{j=1}^m\bigl[\max(0,g_j(x))\bigr]^2.$$

It vanishes exactly on $F$. For $\rho>0$, minimize $\Phi_\rho(x)=f(x)+\rho P(x)$. A violated inequality is charged only for its positive part; squaring the whole $g_j$ would also penalize strictly feasible negative slack and change the intended construction. The positive-part square is continuously differentiable when $g_j$ is smooth, but generally not twice differentiable at the activation boundary.

For $\min x$ subject to $x\ge5$, $g(x)=5-x$ and

$$\Phi_\rho(x)=x+\rho\max(0,5-x)^2.$$

On $x<5$, differentiation gives $1+2\rho(x-5)=0$, so $x_\rho=5-1/(2\rho)$. On $x\ge5$, the best feasible value is five. Substituting $x_\rho$ gives penalized value $5-1/(4\rho)$, less than five, so the global penalized minimizer remains infeasible for every finite $\rho$. With $\rho=100$, its value is $x_\rho=4.995$. An algorithm that waits for exact feasibility can therefore run indefinitely.

The convergence mechanism needs stated conditions. Suppose a feasible global minimizer $x_*$ exists, $f$ is bounded below by $L$ over the region searched, and each penalized problem is solved globally. Then

$$f(x_\rho)+\rho P(x_\rho) \le f(x_*)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(x_\rho)\le\frac{f(x_*)-L}{\rho}.$$

As $\rho\to\infty$, violation vanishes. If a subsequence converges and the functions are continuous, its limit is feasible. The same inequality gives $f(x_\rho)\le f(x_*)$, and continuity makes the feasible limit globally optimal. Existence of a bounded convergent subsequence and global inner solves are substantive hypotheses. Solving nonconvex subproblems locally does not inherit this global conclusion.

Increasing $\rho$ can make a problem ill-conditioned. Near an equality, the Hessian of $\rho h^2$ contains $2\rho\nabla h\nabla h^\top$, producing large curvature normal to the feasible surface without comparable tangential curvature. The numerical solver then faces very different scales. Warm starts help track the path, but do not remove this source of stiffness.

5. Interior barriers approach from the feasible side 08:52

For strictly feasible inequalities, two barrier choices are $-1/g_j(x)$ and $-\log[-g_j(x)]$. Both tend to positive infinity as a constraint approaches zero from below. Their domains exclude the boundary. Define the logarithmic barrier problem

$$\min_x\; f(x)-\mu\sum_{j=1}^m\log[-g_j(x)], \qquad g_j(x)<0,\quad \mu>0.$$

The barrier weight $\mu$ decreases to zero, unlike the exterior quadratic-penalty weight $\rho$, which increases. Keeping separate symbols prevents an easy but damaging parameter-direction error. Equality constraints can be maintained exactly or eliminated; they do not acquire an ordinary logarithmic barrier because an equality has no strictly feasible side.

For our scalar example, the inverse barrier gives $x+\mu/(x-5)$ on $x>5$. Its derivative is $1-\mu/(x-5)^2$, so the minimizer is $x_\mu=5+\sqrt\mu$. The logarithmic barrier gives $x-\mu\log(x-5)$ and minimizer $x_\mu=5+\mu$. Both approach five from above, but at different rates. With $\mu=.1$, the logarithmic path is 5.1; with the same parameter the inverse path is about 5.31623. These values are computed in the lab.

The barrier is not a rule that every unconstrained inner trial must remain finite automatically. A numerical method must restrict steps to its domain, for example by backtracking until every $g_j$ remains negative. A strictly feasible starting point is needed by this simple feasible-start implementation. Phase-I feasibility searches or more general primal-dual algorithms address cases where such a point has not yet been found.

If the feasible set has empty strict interior, the basic inequality barrier may not exist. The constraints $x\le0$ and $-x\le0$ force $x=0$ but admit no point satisfying both strictly. Recognizing their equality structure repairs the formulation. Treating failure to find a strict interior point as proof that the original problem is infeasible would be wrong.

6. Multipliers turn a barrier path into an objective bound 29:50

The following is an original derivation extending the lecture's limit discussion. Assume convex differentiable $f,g_j$, affine equalities $Ax=b$, and an exactly solved logarithmic-barrier subproblem with a strictly feasible solution $x_\mu$. Its stationarity condition can be written

$$\nabla f(x_\mu)+\sum_j\lambda_j\nabla g_j(x_\mu)+A^\top\nu=0, \qquad \lambda_j=\frac{\mu}{-g_j(x_\mu)}>0.$$

Here $\nu$ is the equality multiplier and $\lambda_j[-g_j(x_\mu)]=\mu$ for each inequality. The Lagrangian is convex in $x$, so stationarity makes $x_\mu$ its global minimizer. Its dual value is

$$q(\lambda,\nu)=f(x_\mu)+\sum_j\lambda_jg_j(x_\mu) =f(x_\mu)-m\mu.$$

Weak duality and primal feasibility imply $0\le f(x_\mu)-f_*\le m\mu$. This provides an objective-gap bound for the stated convex setting and exact inner solve, rather than relying only on small changes between iterates. Boyd and Vandenberghe, Chapter 11 develops central paths and barrier-method stopping in the reciprocal parameter convention $t=1/\mu$.

For an approximate inner solve, stationarity residuals matter too. Small $m\mu$ alone does not prove that an arbitrary interior point is close to optimum. Record primal feasibility, equality residual, stationarity residual, complementarity and the available objective bound separately. These quantities answer different questions and expose whether computation stopped because it solved the model or merely exhausted its budget.

The lecture also proposes extrapolating a sequence of barrier points to its parameter-zero limit. If $x_\mu=x_*+a\mu+O(\mu^2)$ and $0<c<1$, then

$$\frac{x_{c\mu}-c x_\mu}{1-c}=x_*+O(\mu^2).$$

Subtracting cancels the linear term. The inverse-barrier example has leading error $\sqrt\mu$, so linear extrapolation in $\mu$ does not supply this cancellation. Fit the correct asymptotic variable, and check feasibility after extrapolation; an extrapolated point is not automatically feasible or optimal.

The laboratory calculates the three scalar paths, verifies the first convex cuts against LP solves, and tests the tangent-direction counterexample. Its fixed inputs and closed-form outputs are deterministic and have no network or filesystem effects. It is a check of the mathematical mechanisms, not a general nonlinear optimization package.

Limitations and six worked problems

Convex supporting planes, feasible straight steps, penalized minima and barrier central points are different objects. Their guarantees require different hypotheses. The calculations here expose those hypotheses before a solver is asked to enforce them.

Exercise 1 — Tangency versus feasibility

At $(1,0)$ on the disk, test $d=(-1,1)$ instead of $(0,1)$.

Worked solution

The directional derivative of $g$ is $-2<0$. Direct substitution gives $g((1,0)+\alpha(-1,1))=-2\alpha+2\alpha^2$. It is nonpositive exactly for $0\le\alpha\le1$. Thus the inward first derivative guarantees a sufficiently short feasible step, and the exact expression identifies its full feasible interval. A length greater than one again violates the disk.

Exercise 2 — A valid cut

Construct the cut for $z^2\le1$ at $z=1.25$ and find the next relaxed maximizer of $z$.

Worked solution

The function value is $1.25^2-1=.5625$, and its derivative is 2.5. The cut is $.5625+2.5(z-1.25)\le0$, hence $z\le1.025$. Every truly feasible $z\in[-1,1]$ satisfies it. Maximizing $z$ in the existing bounded outer approximation returns 1.025. It is still infeasible because $1.025^2>1$, which is permissible for an outer approximation but requires another cut or a stopping tolerance.

Exercise 3 — Exterior accuracy

How large must $\rho$ be for the scalar quadratic-penalty solution to violate $x\ge5$ by at most $.001$?

Worked solution

The exact violation is $5-x_\rho=1/(2\rho)$. Requiring this to be at most $.001$ gives $\rho\ge500$. No finite value makes the violation exactly zero. A feasibility tolerance is part of the numerical specification and should be stated in the physical units of the constraint, rather than hidden in a solver default.

Exercise 4 — A nonsmooth exact penalty

Replace the squared penalty by $\rho\max(0,5-x)$. When does the unconstrained penalized problem have exactly the original minimizer?

Worked solution

For $x<5$, the objective is $5\rho+(1-\rho)x$. If $\rho>1$, this decreases as $x$ increases toward five. On $x\ge5$, the objective is $x$ and increases away from five. The unique global minimizer is therefore five. At $\rho=1$, every $x\le5$ ties, including infeasible points. For $\rho<1$, sending $x\to-\infty$ makes the penalized problem unbounded below. Finite exactness is purchased with nonsmoothness at the boundary; it does not follow for the smooth squared penalty.

Exercise 5 — Barrier gap

For the logarithmic scalar barrier, recover a dual multiplier and the exact primal-dual gap.

Worked solution

At $x_\mu=5+\mu$, the constraint value is $g=-\mu$. Therefore $\lambda=\mu/(-g)=1$. The Lagrangian $x+\lambda(5-x)$ is the constant five, so its infimum is five. The primal value is $5+\mu$, and the gap is $\mu$, agreeing with $m\mu$ for one inequality. This certificate relies on the original objective, not the barrier-augmented value.

Exercise 6 — Extrapolation variable

Use two inverse-barrier points at $\mu$ and $c\mu$ to remove the leading error exactly in the scalar example.

Worked solution

The path is $x_\mu=5+\sqrt\mu$. Let $r=\sqrt c$. Then $x_{c\mu}=5+r\sqrt\mu$, so $(x_{c\mu}-r x_\mu)/(1-r)=5$. The cancellation is linear in $\sqrt\mu$, not in $\mu$. For a general barrier path, such an asymptotic form must be justified or checked. An attractive extrapolation formula does not supply its own regularity hypothesis.

"""Penalty limits, barrier gaps, and valid convex cuts."""
import json
import math

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog


def main():
    parameters = [.1, 1., 10., 100.]
    exterior = [5 - 1 / (2 * rho) for rho in parameters]
    inverse_barrier = [5 + math.sqrt(mu) for mu in parameters]
    log_barrier = [5 + mu for mu in parameters]
    assert all(x < 5 for x in exterior)
    assert all(x > 5 for x in inverse_barrier + log_barrier)
    assert np.isclose(exterior[-1], 4.995)
    assert np.isclose(log_barrier[0], 5.1)
    # Minimize -x on x^2 <= 1, starting with the outer box [-2, 2].
    rows, rhs, iterates = [], [], []
    for _ in range(20):
        result = linprog([-1.], A_ub=rows or None, b_ub=rhs or None,
                         bounds=[(-2., 2.)], method='highs', options={
                             'primal_feasibility_tolerance': 1e-10,
                             'dual_feasibility_tolerance': 1e-10})
        assert result.success
        x = result.x[0]
        iterates.append(float(x))
        if x * x - 1 <= 1e-9:
            break
        # x_k^2 - 1 + 2*x_k*(x-x_k) <= 0.
        rows.append([2 * x])
        rhs.append(x * x + 1)
    assert np.allclose(iterates[:3], [2, 1.25, 1.025])
    assert abs(iterates[-1] - 1) < 1e-8
    # Tangency alone does not make a straight step feasible on a curved set.
    point, direction = np.array([1., 0.]), np.array([0., 1.])
    assert np.dot(2 * point, direction) == 0
    assert np.linalg.norm(point + .01 * direction) ** 2 > 1
    # At a central-path point for min x, x >= 5, gap = mu.
    mu = .01
    x, multiplier = 5 + mu, 1.
    assert np.isclose(multiplier * (x - 5), mu)
    print(json.dumps(dict(parameters=parameters, exterior=exterior,
                          inverse_barrier=inverse_barrier,
                          logarithmic_barrier=log_barrier,
                          cutting_plane_iterates=iterates)))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
